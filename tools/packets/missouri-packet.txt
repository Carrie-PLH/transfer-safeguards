 What is an Ombudsman?
The word “Ombudsman” (om-budz-man) comes from Swedish. It means someone who speaks or helps on behalf of another person. The Missouri Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program is a group of trained volunteers across the state who help residents in long‑term care facilities. Their goal is to make sure residents have a good quality of life and that their rights are protected.
Volunteers are recruited by regional Ombudsman coordinators who work in local programs run by the Area Agencies on Aging (AAAs) or their service partners. After training, Ombudsmen visit residents regularly to discuss any issues. Residents may file complaints anonymously through volunteers, but it will not be acted upon until the resident gives permission. When they act on a complaint, they first support residents in solving the problem themselves. Ombudsmen always act based on what the resident wants, unless a very serious situation requires a different response. Ombudsmen encourage residents to make their own choices and use their rights.
Overall Program Policy
It is important to understand the Ombudsman Program does not have enforcement powers of its own and is not a regulatory agency in any sense. Trust and confidence can be maintained only so long as residents, nursing home facilities, agencies and the public know the Ombudsman has no vested interest in any given case. The Ombudsman's only interest is ensuring long-term care residents are able to freely exercise their rights.
Ombudsman Program Structure
The Missouri Long‑Term Care Ombudsman Program is overseen by the Office of the State Long‑Term Care Ombudsman. This office is part of the Missouri Department of Health and Senior Services (DHSS), within the Division of Senior and Disability Services (DSDS).
The State Office, led by the State Ombudsman, manages and directs all activities of the program.
Missouri law requires DSDS to work with Area Agencies on Aging (AAAs) to run services under the Older Americans Act (OAA), including the Long‑Term Care Ombudsman Program.

 Mission: 
To improve the quality of life for residents in long‑term care facilities through advocacy and education.

 Ombudsman Goals:
• Advocate for residents’ rights.
• Educate residents about their rights and help the community learn about long‑term care issues.
• Empower residents to speak up when they feel unheard.

 Ombudsman Roles
Ombudsmen take on several roles in their work:
Facilitator: Help residents speak up for themselves and learn how to solve their own problems.
Educator: Give information and resources to residents, staff, families, and the community to support learning and problem‑solving.
Broker: Connect residents with people or services that can help and follow up until the issue is solved.
Intermediary: Help improve communication between people who are involved in a problem.
Collaborator: Work with residents and staff to find solutions that meet the resident’s needs.
Mediator: Bring together residents with similar abilities to help them reach an agreement or compromise.
Advocate: Speak or act on behalf of someone else.
Investigator: Gather important information from different sources to understand the problem and find a solution.
Problem solver: Help resolve complaints and issues from residents.
Activist: Work to improve systems and policies that affect residents in long‑term care.

 Systems Advocacy

 Copy link

 The federal Older Americans Act created the Long‑Term Care Ombudsman program and explains what Ombudsmen are required to do. Ombudsmen at the state and regional levels have several jobs, especially in systems advocacy, which means speaking up for residents on a larger, public level.
According to the Older Americans Act, Ombudsmen should:
• Speak up for residents to government agencies and seek solutions that protect their health, safety, and rights.
• Review and comment on laws, rules and government actions that affect residents.
• Help the public share their opinions on laws and policies.
• Support resident and family councils.
• Do any other activities that help protect residents.
To make sure residents’ voices are heard by the public and lawmakers, Ombudsmen also:
• Teach advocacy groups, government agencies and lawmakers how laws and policies affect residents.
• Work to change laws, rules or practices that harm residents’ rights or well‑being.
• Help residents, families and the public share opinions about laws and policies.
• Join work groups that study important issues.
• Take part in public hearings and meetings.
• Provide education in the community.
• Teach other aging‑service providers and the public about long‑term care issues.
